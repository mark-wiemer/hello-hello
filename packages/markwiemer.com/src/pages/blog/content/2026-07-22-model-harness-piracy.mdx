In the three years since the last edition of AI Updates, I've spent thousands of hours at Microsoft on a wide variety of AI tools, including (but very much not limited to) Microsoft Copilot Studio, designed for citizen developers; Microsoft Agent Framework, the successor to Semantic Kernel; Microsoft Fabric data agents, for big data work; declarative agents for Microsoft 365 Copilot, which can handle simpler tasks; custom engine agents for Microsoft 365, designed for complex tasks; and the many surfaces of GitHub Copilot (terminal, panel, window, desktop, and web), which each have their quirks when helping me write code.

In my free time, I continued writing code by hand, then overused my free access to GitHub Copilot Pro and sent my coding friends some slop (sorry!), and have been oscillating back and forth ever since. I paid for two months of an AI coding assistant but will not be renewing for reasons we'll discuss later in this article.

I've adapted an AI-first-and-when-best mindset, actively choosing when to use it and when not to, making many mistakes along the way, and generally learning a ton and saving myself a lot of time on coding tasks. I have never used AI to generate articles, and I never will, because this is a passion project and I hate reading AI prose. I've watched AI become a much-hated technology (maybe it was never loved!), learned of the biggest copyright settlement in history, and read about an AI-generated vaccine that actually works.

Let's discuss what's changed in the past three years.

---

This article mentions Microsoft, my employer and a player in the AI space. I wrote this article in my free time, and all opinions are my own.

---

For about two years after the May 2023 edition of AI Updates, my view of AI was "the more things change, the more they stay the same." We wrote code by hand with the occasional AI autocomplete. Many companies released many models month after month, each allegedly better than the last but none sufficient to actually steal my job. The products we used to build AI-powered features changed, but the capabilities they offered stayed mostly the same. I felt the world around me grasp for uses of this radical new technology and watched mildly impressive demos that never stuck the landing when I actually tried out the products. There was nothing to write about.

There was no single event that changed things. It seems that, late in 2025 and continuing for all of 2026 so far, we just hit an elbow in AI competence. As the graph of perceived quality turned from a hill into a hockey stick, hallucinations decreased, products matured, and I started kicking off AI sessions with natural language and reviewing dozens, then hundreds of lines of AI-generated code. After reviewing tens of thousands of lines of AI code, I've started developing a sense for when to trust AI outputs, which is not something I ever thought I'd say. I still advise everyone, including myself, to never trust AI output. Always verify with a traditional method.

I started looking for a new role in February 2026, and began work on the .NET team in April of 2026. I was then disabled for all of May and I'm still very much recovering. But this shift to a new team and working through a disability has presented a series of opportunities to re-evaluate AI's role (if any) in my day-to-day coding work, and its coincidence with the upswing of AI's S-curve didn't hurt either.
